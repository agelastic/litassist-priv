### Executive Summary

This legal analysis examines the liability of an adventure park operator under the *Civil Liability Act 2002* (NSW) (the Act) following a personal injury caused by a guardrail failure on an elevated rope walk. As LitAssist AI, this advice evaluates the key elements of negligence, the statutory defences of "obvious risk" and "dangerous recreational activity", and the enforceability of contractual waivers in the state of New South Wales.

In NSW, while an elevated rope walk is highly likely to be classified as a "recreational activity" and potentially a "dangerous recreational activity" under the Act, the defence of "obvious risk" under section 5L is unlikely to succeed. The failure of a safety guardrail due to structural or maintenance issues is generally not considered an obvious risk to a reasonable person in the position of the plaintiff. 

Furthermore, while contractual waivers are permitted under section 5N of the Act to exclude liability for recreational activities, their effectiveness depends heavily on precise drafting. A waiver will be strictly construed by NSW courts and may be rendered void if it fails to cover the specific structural failure or if it conflicts with non-excludable statutory guarantees under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL), unless validly limited under federal law.


### Analysis

To establish liability in negligence against the adventure park operator, the plaintiff must first satisfy the general principles of negligence set out in section 5B of the *Civil Liability Act 2002* (NSW). Under section 5B(1), the operator is not negligent in failing to take precautions against a risk of harm unless the risk was foreseeable, the risk was not insignificant, and a reasonable person in the operator's position would have taken those precautions. In the context of an elevated rope walk, the risk of a patron falling is highly foreseeable, and a reasonable operator is expected to inspect, maintain, and ensure the structural integrity of safety barriers such as guardrails.

The operator is likely to raise the defence that the plaintiff was engaged in a "dangerous recreational activity" under section 5L of the Act. Section 5K of the Act defines a "recreational activity" broadly to include any pursuit or activity engaged in for enjoyment, relaxation, or leisure. An elevated rope walk clearly falls within this definition. A "dangerous recreational activity" is defined under section 5K as a recreational activity that involves a "significant risk of physical harm". Whether an activity is a dangerous recreational activity is determined objectively by reference to the general nature of the activity, as established in *Falvo v Oztag Queensland Inc* [2006] HCA 16. An elevated rope walk, by virtue of its height and reliance on safety equipment, would likely be classified as involving a significant risk of physical harm.

Under section 5L(1) of the Act, a defendant is not liable in negligence for harm suffered by a plaintiff as a result of the materialisation of an "obvious risk" of a dangerous recreational activity. Section 5F of the Act defines an "obvious risk" as a risk that, in the circumstances, would have been obvious to a reasonable person in the position of that person. However, NSW courts draw a clear distinction between the inherent risks of an activity (such as losing one's footing on a rope bridge) and risks arising from a failure of safety infrastructure due to negligent maintenance. 

In characterizing the risk, courts look at the specific cause of the accident. In *Action Paintball Games Pty Ltd v Barker*, the court emphasized that the risk must be identified at an appropriate level of generality. The risk of a guardrail structurally failing or collapsing is not an "obvious risk" of an elevated rope walk. A reasonable participant is entitled to assume that the safety infrastructure provided by the commercial operator has been properly maintained and is structurally sound. Therefore, the section 5L defence is highly unlikely to protect the operator from liability for a collapsed guardrail.

The operator may also attempt to rely on a contractual waiver signed by the plaintiff prior to participating. Section 5N of the Act allows a term of a contract for the supply of recreation services to exclude, restrict, or modify the operator's liability for negligence. However, such clauses are subject to strict principles of contractual interpretation. Under the common law doctrine of *contra proferentem*, any ambiguity in a waiver or exclusion clause will be construed strictly against the party seeking to rely on it (the operator). If the waiver only excludes liability for "inherent risks" or "accidents" without explicitly and clearly excluding liability for the operator's own negligence or structural failure of the equipment, the waiver will not protect the operator.

Additionally, the interaction between state civil liability legislation and the Australian Consumer Law (ACL) must be considered. Section 60 of the ACL (which is applied as a law of New South Wales) provides a statutory guarantee that services will be rendered with due care and skill. While section 139A of the *Competition and Consumer Act 2010* (Cth) permits recreational service providers to exclude liability for death or personal injury in relation to these consumer guarantees, the exclusion must be drafted in precise terms and does not apply if the operator's conduct exhibited "reckless conduct" as defined in section 139A(5). If the guardrail failure was the result of egregious neglect or a total absence of inspections, the operator's conduct might cross the threshold into reckless conduct, rendering any waiver void under federal law.


### Conclusion

In conclusion, while the adventure park operator can establish that the elevated rope walk constitutes a dangerous recreational activity under the *Civil Liability Act 2002* (NSW), the operator cannot rely on the "obvious risk" defence under section 5L. The structural failure of a guardrail is not an obvious risk to a reasonable participant. 

The operator's liability will therefore turn on whether they breached their duty of care under section 5B by failing to adequately inspect and maintain the guardrail, and whether the contractual waiver signed by the plaintiff is drafted with sufficient precision to exclude liability for structural negligence. If the waiver is ambiguous or if the operator's maintenance failures amount to reckless conduct under the ACL, the waiver will fail, leaving the operator fully liable for the plaintiff's injuries.
